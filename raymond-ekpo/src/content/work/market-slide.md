Almost every early deck has this slide. A large number, a respectable source, a growth rate, and the line that does the most damage: *if we capture just 1%*.

The founder means it as ambition. The investor reads it as a sign that the founder hasn't worked out who buys. A top-down number describes the size of the world. It says nothing about the size of the company, and nothing about why this team would get any of it.

## What the investor is actually asking

The market slide answers two questions. Could this be big enough to matter to the fund? And does the founder understand exactly who pays, and why?

The second question is the one that builds trust, and a top-down number can't answer it. A bottom-up number can, because building one means showing your working: who the buyer is, how many of them exist, and what they pay.

## Build it from the buyer up

Start with the customer you already sell to and describe them precisely enough that someone else could count them. Multiply by what they actually pay you, not what you hope to charge. That's the market you can reach now, and it's usually smaller than founders want.

That's fine. A small, credible number with an obvious next step is worth more than a large one nobody believes. The size comes from the expansion path: the same buyer in new geographies, the next buyer up, the adjacent product. Show the path in order, and the investor can do the arithmetic on the ambition themselves.

## In the example

The rewritten slide gives up the $100B headline and gains three things. A buyer the investor can picture. A price that comes from real contracts. And an order of expansion that explains how £21M becomes something much larger. Every number on it can be checked in the meeting. That's the point.
