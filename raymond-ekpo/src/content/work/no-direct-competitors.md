It's meant as a strength. The founder has looked, found nothing quite like the product, and wants to say so. But investors have heard it so often that it's become a signal, and not a good one.

If nobody else is doing this, either the problem isn't worth solving or the founder hasn't looked hard enough. Neither makes for a strong meeting.

## The real competitor is what people do today

Every customer is solving the problem somehow already, even if badly. They use a spreadsheet, an agency, a tool built for something else, or a person whose job quietly absorbs it. Sometimes they do nothing at all, which is the hardest competitor to beat.

Naming those alternatives does two things for the narrative. It proves the problem is real, because people are already spending time or money on it. And it shows the founder understands the buyer well enough to know what they'd have to give up to switch.

## Then name the switching moment

The best competition slides don't argue that the product is better on every axis. They identify *when* a customer switches: the event that makes the old way stop working. A team crossing a size threshold, a new regulation, a failed audit, a hire. That moment is a sharper insight than any feature comparison, and it tells the investor exactly how the company finds its next customer.

## In the example

The two-by-two had the company alone in the top-right corner, which persuades nobody. The rewrite lists what clinics actually do today and why each option breaks. Then it names the moment the founder had seen in every early sale: the second practitioner joins, and the paper diary stops working.
