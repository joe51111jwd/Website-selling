The ask is often the last slide written and the least thought through. It states an amount, splits it into a pie chart, and hopes the investor is still too enthusiastic from the previous slides to look closely.

They will look closely. The ask is where the narrative turns into a plan, and a weak one undoes some of the good work earlier in the deck.

## What the money proves

Investors at pre-seed and seed are funding the next set of answers. The question behind the ask is what this round makes true that isn't true today, and whether that will be enough for the next investor to say yes.

So the strongest asks are built backwards from milestones. Here's what we need to have shown to raise the next round. Here's what it takes to show it: the people, the time, the money. Here's how long it lasts. The amount falls out of the plan, instead of the plan being reverse-engineered to fit the amount.

## Percentages aren't a plan

A pie chart of how the money will be spent is the least useful part of most asks. "35% sales and marketing" says nothing about what will be learned. Name the hires instead, the runway in months, and the two or three milestones the round has to hit.

## In the example

The original asked for £1.5M "to accelerate growth". The rewrite asks for less, because the founder worked out what the next round would actually need to see: one sales channel that works on repeat, and proof that larger clinic groups will buy. The smaller number with a clear purpose was the stronger ask.
