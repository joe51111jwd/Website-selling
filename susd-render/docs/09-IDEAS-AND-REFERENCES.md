# SUSD: ideas and reference sites

What's in here (1 Nov 2026): 23 real sites sorted by vibe (all opened and screenshotted today), 15 homepage ideas ranked, and 8 fixes if we keep the water. Phone board with screenshots: `research/board/index.html`.  
**1. Redline:** a real, worn engine gauge. Each hire pushes the needle toward the red; at 13 it is in the red, then a "shift" drops it and the next climb starts higher. It is Michael's own word.  
**2. The Heap:** a real desk. Each hire dumps its new cables on a pile (8 people = 28, 12 = 66, 15 = 105) until the desk is buried, then the same room is tidied into a rack.  
**3. Blackout:** real printed pages painted out with ink until only Michael's sentence is left. You watch Down (pruning) happen, and his words are always the brightest thing on screen.

## Reference sites (23 kept, 12 cut)

Every link below was opened and screenshotted on 1 Nov 2026 (shots in `research/shots/`, phone versions on the board). "For SUSD" is what we would take, not what we would copy.

### Numbers you watch pile up

Scroll stories where a count grows in front of you. This is the closest vibe to "every hire compounds complexity": you feel it before you read it.

- **Reuters: Drowning in plastic (2019)**: https://www.reuters.com/graphics/ENVIRONMENT-PLASTIC/0100B275155/  
  A scroll story that piles up the plastic bottles sold per hour, day, month, year and decade. Each pile stands beside a landmark you know: Christ the Redeemer, the Eiffel Tower, the Burj Khalifa, Manhattan.  
  *For SUSD:* One hire per scroll step. Each step is a bigger pile beside something a founder knows, with the count huge: 8 people, 28. 12, 66. 15, 105.
- **Reuters: A torrent of trash (2024)**: https://www.reuters.com/graphics/NORTHKOREA-SOUTHKOREA/TRASH/klvynygmjpg/  
  North Korea's trash balloons arrive wave by wave as you scroll, one big count per wave (3,500, then 330, 310...), until the sky is crowded.  
  *For SUSD:* The screen clogs up one step at a time. Near things are big and sharp, far things small and pale, so the depth comes from the scene, not from drop shadows.
- **The Pudding: This is a teenager (2024)**: https://pudding.cool/2024/03/teenagers/  
  It opens on one small figure ("This is a teenager"). Then hundreds of figures, each a real person, sort into rows while a year ruler moves along the bottom.  
  *For SUSD:* Start with the founder alone, then cut to the team. A ruler along the bottom that moves with your scroll, showing team size instead of years.
- **The Pudding: Population Mountains (2018)**: https://pudding.cool/2018/12/3d-cities-story/  
  A flat dot map of New York grows into 3D spikes as the camera tilts. The same data suddenly has weight.  
  *For SUSD:* The moment flat turns 3D is the book's own argument: one-dimensional growth is a line, three dimensions give it volume. One scroll step where a flat drawing gains mass.
- **The Pudding: The Real Kevin Bacon (Oct 2026)**: https://pudding.cool/2026/10/kevin-bacon/  
  A network of actors where one route lights up at a time, with a one-line caption naming the pair ("Zendaya: two movies away from Kevin Bacon.").  
  *For SUSD:* How to show 28 or 66 pathways without a hairball: light one pair per step ("you and your bookkeeper") and keep the rest faint. Not the dots-on-purple look.
- **AI 2027**: https://ai-2027.com/  
  A long essay with a panel in the margin that updates as you read. Its field of small squares grows from 2,000 copies to 330K.  
  *For SUSD:* Michael's lines in the column, and a margin instrument that thickens with each hire. Take the mechanism only; its cream-and-small-labels look is on our banned list.
- **Moody's: US office vacancy (2024)**: https://www.moodys.com/web/en/us/insights/data-stories/us-commercial-real-estate-vacancies-downtown-vs-suburbs.html  
  A full-screen wall of photographed office windows, some lit and some dark. A huge city name and its vacancy figure change in place every few seconds.  
  *For SUSD:* A real photo you read as data (lit windows you could count), with one huge line of type that still wins over the photo. That balance is what James asked for.

### Made by hand, and you can tell

Ink, pencil and real material. The strongest answer to "looks AI made" is proof that a person made it.

- **A Humument (Tom Phillips)**: https://www.tomphillips.co.uk/works/humument  
  Phillips painted over every page of an 1892 novel, on and off for over 50 years, leaving only a few words on each page. The site shows the original page beside his versions.  
  *For SUSD:* Down (pruning) that you can watch: a full page of words, nearly all covered, and what is left is the message. Only works as real ink, photographed.
- **Long Now: Pace Layering**: https://longnow.org/ideas/pace-layers/  
  The page opens on Stewart Brand's 1996 notebook sketch of the idea, with his own handwritten date and place.  
  *For SUSD:* If Michael has a first sketch of Up / Side / Down (notebook, whiteboard photo), show it with a plain caption: who drew it, when, where.
- **Crafting Interpreters (Robert Nystrom)**: https://craftinginterpreters.com/  
  A programming book whose header is one hand-inked map of the whole book. The web edition is free.  
  *For SUSD:* Two lines that let readers pick themselves ("If so, this book is for you."), and one honest "free, really" line next to the Bootloader button.
- **Conzett Bronzini (Swiss engineers)**: https://cbp.ch/  
  A structural engineering firm. One slide is a scanned pencil calculation sheet for LACMA, dated, with force figures written by hand.  
  *For SUSD:* Show the pathway sum worked by hand (8 = 28, 12 = 66, 15 = 105) on a real scanned sheet. It reads as checked, not marketed.
- **The Pudding: Dicing an Onion (2025)**: https://pudding.cool/2025/08/onions/  
  The big words are spelled in letters made from cut onion slices, one image per letter.  
  *For SUSD:* This is "make your own font" done for real: a few key words spelled in a material that carries the message (thread, cable, steel). Skip the cute tone.

### The real object, photographed

Books and printed things shot in real light. One object, one sentence, nothing faked in CSS.

- **Irma Boom Office**: https://www.irmaboom.nl/  
  A book designer's site: one full-screen close-up photo of a printed book (an edge, a page, a spine), a new one each visit, and tiny contact text. No menu.  
  *For SUSD:* A close-up of the real printed book or Bootloader as the ground for one sentence. Real paper in real light.
- **Lars Müller Publishers**: https://www.lars-mueller-publishers.com/  
  A Swiss publisher. Its headline "New Books for FALL 2026" has photos of real books sitting inside the line at the height of the letters.  
  *For SUSD:* Put the real object inside the sentence at word size, so you cannot look at it without reading. One object per line at most.
- **Standards Manual**: https://standardsmanual.com/  
  A publisher that reissues old design manuals (the NYC subway, NASA). Each book is a big photo of the real thing with one quote set large beside it.  
  *For SUSD:* Michael wants the Bootloader seen as a spec, not a prompt. Photograph it printed and bound like an operating manual, with one line beside it.

### Load, structure and gauges

Engineering and instruments. Michael's own pictures (Redline, the building with only height) live here.

- **OFFICE Kersten Geers David Van Severen**: https://officekgdvs.com/  
  A Belgian architecture firm. Each project is one screen: a photo on one half, the building's ink plan on the other, one caption.  
  *For SUSD:* The height you can see beside the structure you can't. It stages "You can't build a building with only height." without a word of explanation.
- **V&A blog: the Forth Bridge "human cantilever"**: https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/projects/building-bridges-models-creating-confidence-in-engineering-innovation  
  In 1887 the bridge engineers explained their design with three men, two chairs and some timber. The man in the middle sits relaxed because the structure carries him.  
  *For SUSD:* A team carrying load because of its shape, in one photo. We would restage it with a real small team and shoot it ourselves.
- **Gordon Murray Automotive**: https://www.gordonmurray.com/  
  A maker of light supercars. Close-up detail photos, then single sentences alone on black ("weight is the enemy of everything"). Its T.50 has a big analogue rev counter with a red line.  
  *For SUSD:* The rhythm: one real detail, then one sentence alone. And the redline as a real object. Not the glossy car-ad styling.
- **Dezeen: Gaudí's hanging model (2026)**: https://www.dezeen.com/2026/06/16/colonia-guell-hanging-models-gaudi-centenary/  
  An article on how Gaudí designed a church as strings and weights hung upside down, read in a mirror. Change one string and every curve moves.  
  *For SUSD:* A real model where adding one weight shifts the whole web, the way one hire shifts everyone's work. The page itself is full of ads; the idea is the reference.
- **Low-tech Magazine (solar edition)**: https://solar.lowtechmagazine.com/  
  A solar-powered site. Its background is filled up to the server battery's charge (48% when we looked), and it says so at the top.  
  *For SUSD:* The page itself can be the gauge: margins or spacing that tighten as the team grows. Not a rising fill, which is the Waterline again.

### Asking for the email

How others ask for an email or give a book away without it feeling like a funnel. Plus the competitor to avoid.

- **Craig Mod**: https://craigmod.com/  
  A writer whose homepage opens with the newsletter sign-up and one honest line: "Some ~50,000 subscribers. Always one-click to unsubscribe."  
  *For SUSD:* The tone under the Bootloader button: what the file is (version 16, 35,445 words), what the emails are, how to leave.
- **Butterick's Practical Typography**: https://practicaltypography.com/  
  A whole book, free on the web, with a "Typography in ten minutes" front door and a polite chapter called "Please pay for this book".  
  *For SUSD:* Give first, ask second, and offer a short way into a big text. Borrow the manners, not the plain look.
- **EOS Organizational Checkup (what to avoid)**: https://organizationalcheckup.com/  
  The free assessment behind the Traction business books: "20 targeted questions" and "One clear score", with 760,000+ assessments.  
  *For SUSD:* The nearest competitor to the Bootloader. Match how clearly it says what you get back. Avoid everything else: pill tag, gradient button, score dial, stat row.

## Ideas, ranked (15)

The scouts sent 17. Three pairs were the same idea and are merged; one idea that keeps the water (Loaded) is added so it can be ranked against the rest. Only Loaded keeps the water. Michael's lines in quotes are word for word.

### 1. Redline (no water)

**What you see:** A close-up of a real, worn instrument panel: two analogue dials under glass. The left reads coordination pathways, its scale printed 0, 1, 3, 6, 10 ... 28 ... 66, red from 78. The right reads capacity. Each scroll step adds one person: the left needle swings further every time while the right one creeps. At 13 it is in the red. Then the page shifts: strain drops back as systems take the load, and the next climb starts from a higher capacity. Three cycles, then calm.  
**With no clicks:** The dial's own scale is the pathway sum, so you see each hire cost more than the last without reading a chart. Michael's lines sit at the two ends: "Complexity > Capacity = Redline." and "Capacity > Complexity = Oxygen." Each shift is one Up, Side, Down.  
**The Bootloader click:** The last frame is the panel at rest with an empty readout window and "How many people are on your team?". One button: get the Bootloader to find where your needle sits. The book link sits under it.  
**What it takes:** Buy a real used gauge ($50 to $150), print our own dial faces, shoot each needle position on a locked camera (2 to 3 days). Or model it in Blender, with the needles as separate layers turned by code (4 to 5 days). About 2 days of page code.  
**Main risk:** It can read as a car ad or a game screen. It must be a matte, worn engine-room or aircraft panel, never a sports car and never glowing. The gear-shift reading of the cycle is ours, not Michael's, so check it with him.  
**Looks to:** Gordon Murray Automotive, AI 2027.  
*Our type system already has B612, a face drawn for Airbus cockpit instruments.*

### 2. The Heap (no water)

**What you see:** A real small office: one desk, one chair, the founder. Each scroll step adds one person to a row along the wall and drops that hire's new cables on a pile by the desk, one cable per pathway, with the count huge: "8 people. 28." "12. 66." "15. 105." The pile buries the desk, then the chair, then reaches the door. The last beat is the same room tidied: cables bundled into three trunks (Side), dead ones unplugged (Down, never people), a neat rack with room for one more.  
**With no clicks:** The row grows by one, the pile by more every time. You feel "Every hire compounds complexity before it compounds capacity." without reading it. The desk is the landmark, the way the Eiffel Tower is for Reuters.  
**The Bootloader click:** The final frame is the tidy rack with one empty, labelled port and "How many people are on your team?". The button gets the file (version 16, 35,445 words). The book link sits under it.  
**What it takes:** Real stop-motion photos: about 105 cheap patch cables, a desk and chair, a locked camera, 10 states. A day to shoot, a day to edit, under $200. Or a Blender cable simulation (1 to 2 days of renders). About 2 days of page code.  
**Main risk:** Cables can read as "IT department" instead of people talking, so the first step must name one cable as one pair of people. If the tidy ending doesn't land, it is just a mess.  
**Looks to:** Reuters: Drowning in plastic (2019), Reuters: A torrent of trash (2024), The Pudding: The Real Kevin Bacon (Oct 2026).  
*No water, but the same drowning feeling.*

### 3. Blackout (no water)

**What you see:** A full-screen photo of a real printed page of the book in daylight, every word painted out in black ink except one sentence: "Every hire compounds complexity before it compounds capacity." Scrolling plays the ink going on, page after page, so a wall of text is pruned to one line. Then "Past twelve, something breaks.", then "Up. Side. Down. Repeat.". The last page is a page of the Bootloader, inked down to its first question.  
**With no clicks:** On every screen the only readable words are Michael's, so they always win. Down (pruning) is not explained; you watch it happen.  
**The Bootloader click:** The last words left are "How many people are on your team?", with the button right under them. The file is the one thing left that speaks to you.  
**What it takes:** Print 5 or 6 real pages of the manuscript. Someone, ideally Michael, paints them out with brush and ink. Shoot about 100 frames on a copy stand in daylight (2 to 3 days). 3 to 4 days to build, with tight phone crops. No Blender.  
**Main risk:** James may see Corrected Edition again (marks on printed text), or a redacted-document meme. It only works with real ink from a real hand, photographed. A whole page can't be read on a phone, so each page needs a close crop.  
**Looks to:** A Humument (Tom Phillips), Irma Boom Office.  

### 4. Only Height (no water)

**What you see:** A slim office tower at dusk. Every lit window is one pathway, not one person. Each hire adds a floor and lights that hire's new windows, so the lit area grows much faster than the height. By 13 the facade blazes and the tower leans under its load. Then the same plot is rebuilt the Up-Side-Down way: a wider base and a core (Side), windows lit for nothing go dark (Down), and it climbs again. It can also be an architect's drawing set (elevation, plan, section) in black ink beside a photo of the finished building.  
**With no clicks:** It is Michael's best line, staged: "You can't build a building with only height. You need all three dimensions or it topples." One huge line of type sits over the facade, as on Moody's.  
**The Bootloader click:** The wide building's ground-floor door is dark. The button is the door, and the one dramatic moment is its light coming on. The Bootloader tells you which building you are in.  
**What it takes:** A Blender facade whose window lights switch from a table (2 to 3 days of renders), or a physical model photographed (2 to 3 weeks and a model maker). About 2 days of code.  
**Main risk:** It could read as "blocks again". The tower must fail because the load outgrew its base, not because it is tall. Down must be lights going out on waste, never floors (people) removed. The type has to beat the picture.  
**Looks to:** Moody's: US office vacancy (2024), OFFICE Kersten Geers David Van Severen, The Pudding: Population Mountains (2018).  
*Merged with the scouts' drawing-set idea (elevation, plan, section).*

### 5. The Human Cantilever (no water)

**What you see:** A black-and-white photo shot for real in a workshop: a small modern team restages the 1887 Forth Bridge demonstration. Two people on chairs hold timber struts, the founder sits relaxed on a plank slung between them, bricks anchor the ends, and Michael's sentence is pinned to the wall behind. Scrolling moves through the same shoot: the founder alone holding everything up, eight people tangled with nobody carrying anything, then the proper cantilever, then one more seat added.  
**With no clicks:** One photo says "The business can breathe. Systems carry what the business creates.": the person in the middle is relaxed because the structure carries them. The tangle shows more people, more pathways, less carrying.  
**The Bootloader click:** It ends on: find out what your team is carrying and where the load goes, then the button.  
**What it takes:** One studio shoot with 6 to 10 people (or Michael's real team), chairs, timber, bricks and one hard light. Photographer and releases about $3,000 to $6,000. No Blender. About 2 days of code; a week in all.  
**Main risk:** It can look like a team-building ad or a costume gimmick, and flat light makes it look like stock. Faces pull the eye, so the sentence on the wall must be huge. Needs a credit line for the original.  
**Looks to:** V&A blog: the Forth Bridge "human cantilever", OFFICE Kersten Geers David Van Severen.  

### 6. Pins and Thread (no water)

**What you see:** Real brass pins (people) and fine thread (pathways), shot from above in window light. Two ways to stage it. One board, where each scroll pushes in a pin and runs thread to every pin already there: at 8 a web, at 15 a pin pops out. Or a glass-topped specimen drawer with one row per team size (1, 3, 7, 12, 14, 15) and a handwritten label, so you find your own size on the way down. Both end with the threads re-strung through three rings (Side) and loose ones snipped (Down), every pin still in place.  
**With no clicks:** Every thread can be counted, so the sum is something you watch. Coordination in real thread is one of Michael's own pictures (01-MEANING ranks it third).  
**The Bootloader click:** An empty row or pinhole with a blank label and "How many people are on your team?". The Bootloader fills in your label.  
**What it takes:** A cork board or specimen drawer ($100 to $300), pins, thread, rings. An afternoon to string 105 threads; 1 to 2 days to make, 1 day to shoot. About 2 days of code.  
**Main risk:** Red string on cork is the conspiracy-wall cliché, and our earlier thread idea was turned down as "a lot going on". Pins are never pulled out, which would read as layoffs.  
**Looks to:** The Pudding: The Real Kevin Bacon (Oct 2026), The Pudding: This is a teenager (2024).  
*Merged: the scouts' Specimen Drawer and String Board.*

### 10. Hang It Upside Down (no water)

### 11. Two Companies, Ten Years (no water)

### 12. Lock-Up (letterpress) (no water)

**What you see:** Overhead photos of a press bed with Michael's sentence set in real metal and wood type. More lines of type are added, one per hire. Lifted without being locked up, the type spills across the stone (printers call it "pie"). Then wooden blocks and steel wedges are tightened round it (Side) and it lifts as one solid block. After the run the type goes back in the case (Down) and the next job is set.  
**With no clicks:** Printing already runs in the book's cycle (set, lock up, put away, repeat) and has a word for toppling. Five or six real photos carry the whole argument.  
**The Bootloader click:** Printers pull a proof before a run. The Bootloader is that proof for your team, before you add the next line.  
**What it takes:** A 1 to 2 day shoot in a working letterpress shop: photographer, type and shop hire about $2,000 to $4,000. 2 days of code.  
**Main risk:** Letterpress and paper drift straight into the cream, nostalgic AI look unless it is shot as an industrial shop: black ink, steel, hard light. It can feel crafty instead of about running a team.  
**Looks to:** Hatch Show Print.  

### 13. Freestanding Height (tower crane) (no water)

**What you see:** Dawn over a half-built concrete core with a tower crane beside it, small in the frame, Michael's building line filling the sky. Each scroll adds one mast section (a hire). Past its safe height the crane sways and the cab gauge goes red. Then a collar ties the mast to the finished floors (Side), the crane climbs and removes the sections it no longer needs (Down), and the next climb starts from a taller building.  
**With no clicks:** Everyone knows a tall crane can fall over. The scroll shows growth only upward until it is unsafe, then growth that is safe because it is tied in. Real cranes work this way: Liebherr's Auckland crane was tied to its building nine times.  
**The Bootloader click:** Find your freestanding height: the Bootloader works out how far your team can climb before it needs to tie in.  
**What it takes:** A Blender crane and core, about 150 frames, 4 to 5 days. Or a physical model, 2 to 3 weeks.  
**Main risk:** Construction pictures slide into stock or real-estate looks, the crane steals the eye, and "climbing down" is a weak picture of Down. Cranes also say "build big", which the book doesn't.  
**Looks to:** OFFICE Kersten Geers David Van Severen, Liebherr.

## If we keep the water

James said he isn't fully set on the water. If we keep it, these changes would make the ship's side work better. They come from the current renders, the 31 Oct feedback ("looks like a sewer") and the open items in the build notes.

1. **Show that it is a ship.** Every frame is a flat dark wall over water, edge to edge, so nothing says "ship" and it reads as a wall again. Turn the camera so the hull curves away toward the bow, with a strip of sky and the deck rail at the top and a mooring line in frame. One look and you know where you are.
2. **Show why the water rises.** Right now it climbs with no cause. Lower one container onto the deck per hire, at the top of the frame, so the ship visibly sits deeper. Down is containers lifted off and the ship rising, which also keeps Down from looking like a fall.
3. **Calm the reflection.** The striped reflection of the letters is the biggest busy area on the first screen and fights the sentence. Flatter water or a slightly higher camera, so the water reads as clear depth, not stripes.
4. **Make the count big and the gauge readable.** The draught marks are tiny at the right edge. Pin one big count to the waterline at each step (8 people, 28 pathways) and paint the 12 load line large enough to read from across a room. The water's job is to reach that mark.
5. **One drowned beat, not three.** The underwater frames are flat teal and read as a swimming pool. Keep one short beat at Redline, then come straight back up.
6. **A real day.** The stills are blue-grey and dusky. Use mid-morning harbour light: blue sky in the reflection, white letters, the red bottom paint showing, a bit of quay. More photograph, less render.
7. **Fewer words on the first screen.** It carries about 20 separate bits of small text around the sentence. Keep the sentence, who it is for, one number pair and one button. The rest arrives on scroll.
8. **Check it beside a real photo first.** Before rendering any sequence, put the hero next to a real photo of a ship's side with draught marks. Or photograph a real hull in a harbour and add the words in post. If ours doesn't hold up beside the photo, stop there.
