1

Redline

No water

A close-up of a real, worn instrument panel: two analogue dials under glass. The left reads coordination pathways, its scale printed 0, 1, 3, 6, 10 ... 28 ... 66, red from 78. The right reads capacity. Each scroll step adds one person: the left needle swings further every time while the right one creeps. At 13 it is in the red. Then the page shifts: strain drops back as systems take the load, and the next climb starts from a higher capacity. Three cycles, then calm.

With no clicks
The dial’s own scale is the pathway sum, so you see each hire cost more than the last without reading a chart. Michael’s lines sit at the two ends: “Complexity > Capacity = Redline.” and “Capacity > Complexity = Oxygen.” Each shift is one Up, Side, Down.
The Bootloader click
The last frame is the panel at rest with an empty readout window and “How many people are on your team?”. One button: get the Bootloader to find where your needle sits. The book link sits under it.
What it takes
Buy a real used gauge ($50 to $150), print our own dial faces, shoot each needle position on a locked camera (2 to 3 days). Or model it in Blender, with the needles as separate layers turned by code (4 to 5 days). About 2 days of page code.
Main risk
It can read as a car ad or a game screen. It must be a matte, worn engine-room or aircraft panel, never a sports car and never glowing. The gear-shift reading of the cycle is ours, not Michael’s, so check it with him.

Our type system already has B612, a face drawn for Airbus cockpit instruments.

Looks to: Gordon Murray Automotive, AI 2027

The Heap

No water

A real small office: one desk, one chair, the founder. Each scroll step adds one person to a row along the wall and drops that hire’s new cables on a pile by the desk, one cable per pathway, with the count huge: “8 people. 28.” “12. 66.” “15. 105.” The pile buries the desk, then the chair, then reaches the door. The last beat is the same room tidied: cables bundled into three trunks (Side), dead ones unplugged (Down, never people), a neat rack with room for one more.

With no clicks
The row grows by one, the pile by more every time. You feel “Every hire compounds complexity before it compounds capacity.” without reading it. The desk is the landmark, the way the Eiffel Tower is for Reuters.
The Bootloader click
The final frame is the tidy rack with one empty, labelled port and “How many people are on your team?”. The button gets the file (version 16, 35,445 words). The book link sits under it.
What it takes
Real stop-motion photos: about 105 cheap patch cables, a desk and chair, a locked camera, 10 states. A day to shoot, a day to edit, under $200. Or a Blender cable simulation (1 to 2 days of renders). About 2 days of page code.
Main risk
Cables can read as “IT department” instead of people talking, so the first step must name one cable as one pair of people. If the tidy ending doesn’t land, it is just a mess.

No water, but the same drowning feeling.

Looks to: Reuters: Drowning in plastic (2019), Reuters: A torrent of trash (2024), The Pudding: The Real Kevin Bacon (Oct 2026)

Blackout

No water

A full-screen photo of a real printed page of the book in daylight, every word painted out in black ink except one sentence: “Every hire compounds complexity before it compounds capacity.” Scrolling plays the ink going on, page after page, so a wall of text is pruned to one line. Then “Past twelve, something breaks.”, then “Up. Side. Down. Repeat.”. The last page is a page of the Bootloader, inked down to its first question.

With no clicks
On every screen the only readable words are Michael’s, so they always win. Down (pruning) is not explained; you watch it happen.
The Bootloader click
The last words left are “How many people are on your team?”, with the button right under them. The file is the one thing left that speaks to you.
What it takes
Print 5 or 6 real pages of the manuscript. Someone, ideally Michael, paints them out with brush and ink. Shoot about 100 frames on a copy stand in daylight (2 to 3 days). 3 to 4 days to build, with tight phone crops. No Blender.
Main risk
James may see Corrected Edition again (marks on printed text), or a redacted-document meme. It only works with real ink from a real hand, photographed. A whole page can’t be read on a phone, so each page needs a close crop.

Looks to: A Humument (Tom Phillips), Irma Boom Office

Only Height

No water

A slim office tower at dusk. Every lit window is one pathway, not one person. Each hire adds a floor and lights that hire’s new windows, so the lit area grows much faster than the height. By 13 the facade blazes and the tower leans under its load. Then the same plot is rebuilt the Up-Side-Down way: a wider base and a core (Side), windows lit for nothing go dark (Down), and it climbs again. It can also be an architect’s drawing set (elevation, plan, section) in black ink beside a photo of the finished building.

With no clicks
It is Michael’s best line, staged: “You can’t build a building with only height. You need all three dimensions or it topples.” One huge line of type sits over the facade, as on Moody’s.
The Bootloader click
The wide building’s ground-floor door is dark. The button is the door, and the one dramatic moment is its light coming on. The Bootloader tells you which building you are in.
What it takes
A Blender facade whose window lights switch from a table (2 to 3 days of renders), or a physical model photographed (2 to 3 weeks and a model maker). About 2 days of code.
Main risk
It could read as “blocks again”. The tower must fail because the load outgrew its base, not because it is tall. Down must be lights going out on waste, never floors (people) removed. The type has to beat the picture.

Merged with the scouts’ drawing-set idea (elevation, plan, section).

Looks to: Moody’s: US office vacancy (2024), OFFICE Kersten Geers David Van Severen, The Pudding: Population Mountains (2018)

The Human Cantilever

No water

A black-and-white photo shot for real in a workshop: a small modern team restages the 1887 Forth Bridge demonstration. Two people on chairs hold timber struts, the founder sits relaxed on a plank slung between them, bricks anchor the ends, and Michael’s sentence is pinned to the wall behind. Scrolling moves through the same shoot: the founder alone holding everything up, eight people tangled with nobody carrying anything, then the proper cantilever, then one more seat added.

With no clicks
One photo says “The business can breathe. Systems carry what the business creates.”: the person in the middle is relaxed because the structure carries them. The tangle shows more people, more pathways, less carrying.
The Bootloader click
It ends on: find out what your team is carrying and where the load goes, then the button.
What it takes
One studio shoot with 6 to 10 people (or Michael’s real team), chairs, timber, bricks and one hard light. Photographer and releases about $3,000 to $6,000. No Blender. About 2 days of code; a week in all.
Main risk
It can look like a team-building ad or a costume gimmick, and flat light makes it look like stock. Faces pull the eye, so the sentence on the wall must be huge. Needs a credit line for the original.

Looks to: V&A blog: the Forth Bridge “human cantilever”, OFFICE Kersten Geers David Van Severen

Pins and Thread

No water

Real brass pins (people) and fine thread (pathways), shot from above in window light. Two ways to stage it. One board, where each scroll pushes in a pin and runs thread to every pin already there: at 8 a web, at 15 a pin pops out. Or a glass-topped specimen drawer with one row per team size (1, 3, 7, 12, 14, 15) and a handwritten label, so you find your own size on the way down. Both end with the threads re-strung through three rings (Side) and loose ones snipped (Down), every pin still in place.

With no clicks
Every thread can be counted, so the sum is something you watch. Coordination in real thread is one of Michael’s own pictures (01-MEANING ranks it third).
The Bootloader click
An empty row or pinhole with a blank label and “How many people are on your team?”. The Bootloader fills in your label.
What it takes
A cork board or specimen drawer ($100 to $300), pins, thread, rings. An afternoon to string 105 threads; 1 to 2 days to make, 1 day to shoot. About 2 days of code.
Main risk
Red string on cork is the conspiracy-wall cliché, and our earlier thread idea was turned down as “a lot going on”. Pins are never pulled out, which would read as layoffs.

Merged: the scouts’ Specimen Drawer and String Board.

Looks to: The Pudding: The Real Kevin Bacon (Oct 2026), The Pudding: This is a teenager (2024)

The Author’s Hand

No water

Michael’s sentence set large and fixed at the top. Below it, an overhead daylight film of a real hand and pen on paper, moved by your scroll. It writes the napkin maths (3 people, 3 lines; 8, 28; 12, 66; 15, 105), joins 8 dots so 28 lines become a knot, then turns the page and draws the cycle, each loop ending higher. With no clicks

The Bootloader click

What it takes
Half a day of filming (Michael or a hand double), one take, 4K, overhead. 300 to 400 frames for the scroll. About 1 day to shoot, 3 to build.
Main risk

Lock-Up (letterpress)

No water

Overhead photos of a press bed with Michael’s sentence set in real metal and wood type. More lines of type are added, one per hire. Lifted without being locked up, the type spills across the stone (printers call it “pie”). Then wooden blocks and steel wedges are tightened round it (Side) and it lifts as one solid block. After the run the type goes back in the case (Down) and the next job is set.

With no clicks
Printing already runs in the book’s cycle (set, lock up, put away, repeat) and has a word for toppling. Five or six real photos carry the whole argument.
The Bootloader click
Printers pull a proof before a run. The Bootloader is that proof for your team, before you add the next line.

If we keep the water

James said he isn’t fully set on the water. If we keep it, these changes would make the ship’s side work better. They come from the current renders, the 31 Oct feedback (“looks like a sewer”) and the open items in the build notes.

  1. Show that it is a ship. Every frame is a flat dark wall over water, edge to edge, so nothing says “ship” and it reads as a wall again. Turn the camera so the hull curves away toward the bow, with a strip of sky and the deck rail at the top and a mooring line in frame. One look and you know where you are.

  2. Show why the water rises. Right now it climbs with no cause. Lower one container onto the deck per hire, at the top of the frame, so the ship visibly sits deeper. Down is containers lifted off and the ship rising, which also keeps Down from looking like a fall.

  3. Calm the reflection. The striped reflection of the letters is the biggest busy area on the first screen and fights the sentence. Flatter water or a slightly higher camera, so the water reads as clear depth, not stripes.

  4. Make the count big and the gauge readable. The draught marks are tiny at the right edge. Pin one big count to the waterline at each step (8 people, 28 pathways) and paint the 12 load line large enough to read from across a room. The water’s job is to reach that mark.

  5. One drowned beat, not three. The underwater frames are flat teal and read as a swimming pool. Keep one short beat at Redline, then come straight back up.

  6. A real day. The stills are blue-grey and dusky. Use mid-morning harbour light: blue sky in the reflection, white letters, the red bottom paint showing, a bit of quay. More photograph, less render.

  7. Fewer words on the first screen. It carries about 20 separate bits of small text around the sentence. Keep the sentence, who it is for, one number pair and one button. The rest arrives on scroll.

  8. Check it beside a real photo first. Before rendering any sequence, put the hero next to a real photo of a ship’s side with draught marks. Or photograph a real hull in a harbour and add the words in post. If ours doesn’t hold up beside the photo, stop there.
